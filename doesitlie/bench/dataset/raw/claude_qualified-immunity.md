# The Current Doctrine of Qualified Immunity for Police Officers in 42 U.S.C. § 1983 Cases

## Details

Qualified immunity shields police officers sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 from damages unless they violated "clearly established" law — a two-prong test under which courts may decide either prong first, and the right must be defined with great specificity. (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/457/800/)

42 U.S.C. § 1983 makes "[e]very person" who, under color of state law, deprives another of constitutional rights "liable to the party injured" — it contains no text creating any immunity. (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1983)

The modern "clearly established law" standard was created in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982), replacing the older subjective good-faith test with a purely objective one. (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/457/800/)

Courts require the plaintiff to identify a prior case — typically on closely analogous facts — placing the constitutional question "beyond debate." (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-1668_19m2.pdf)

A right is clearly established only when it is "sufficiently clear that every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-1539_09m1.pdf) The Court draws this from Mullenix v. Luna, 577 U.S. 7 (2015). (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-1539_09m1.pdf)

The Court has described the doctrine as protecting "all but the plainly incompetent or those who knowingly violate the law" (Malley v. Briggs). (https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/fair-courts-e-lert-us-supreme-court-declines-hear-qualified-immunity)

The Court has "repeatedly told courts — and the Ninth Circuit in particular — not to define clearly established law at a high level of generality," drawing on Ashcroft v. al-Kidd, 563 U.S. 731 (2011). (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/17-467/) This requirement traces to Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 (1987). (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/483/635/)

The relevant precedent must define the right with a "high degree of specificity" (District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583 U.S. 48 (2018)). (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/25-297)

Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 (1982), decided 8-1 (Justice Powell writing; Chief Justice Burger dissenting), held that "government officials performing discretionary functions generally are shielded from liability for civil damages insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." (https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/supreme-court-case-library/harlow-v-fitzgerald) It abandoned the prior subjective "good faith" inquiry in favor of "objective legal reasonableness." (https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/457/800.html)

Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 (1987). Justice Scalia, writing for the Court, established that "the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/483/635/)

Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194 (2001), decided June 18, 2001, held that the qualified immunity inquiry is "not susceptible of fusion with the question whether unreasonable force was used in making the arrest" and mandated a rigid two-step sequence. (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/99-1977)

Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730 (2002). In a 6-3 decision (Justice Stevens writing), the Court denied immunity to Alabama guards who handcuffed inmate Larry Hope to a "hitching post" in the sun for seven hours, holding officials "can be on notice that their conduct violates established law even in novel factual circumstances." (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/730/)

Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223 (2009). Justice Alito, writing for a unanimous Court, made the Saucier sequence discretionary rather than mandatory, holding judges "should be permitted to exercise their sound discretion in deciding which of the two prongs of the qualified immunity analysis should be addressed first." (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/555/223/)

Taylor v. Riojas, 592 U.S. 7 (2020). In a per curiam decision on November 2, 2020, the Court reversed the Fifth Circuit's grant of immunity to officers who confined inmate Trent Taylor for six days in cells covered "nearly floor to ceiling" in feces and then raw sewage. (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1261_bq7c.pdf) It was the first time the Court relied on the obviousness of a violation to overturn a grant of qualified immunity. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_v._Riojas)

McCoy v. Alamu, 141 S. Ct. 1364 (2021). On a GVR order (February 2021), the Court vacated the Fifth Circuit's grant of immunity to a guard who pepper-sprayed inmate Prince McCoy in the face, remanding for reconsideration in light of Taylor v. Riojas. (https://www.macarthurjustice.org/case/mccoy-v-alamu/)

Rivas-Villegas v. Cortesluna, 595 U.S. 1 (2021). In a unanimous per curiam decision on October 18, 2021, the Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and granted immunity to a Union City officer who briefly placed his knee on the back of an armed suspect, finding the sole cited precedent "materially distinguishable." (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-1539_09m1.pdf)

City of Tahlequah v. Bond, 595 U.S. 9 (2021). In a unanimous per curiam decision the same day, the Court granted immunity to officers who fatally shot Dominic Rollice as he raised a hammer, holding "the officers plainly did not violate any clearly established law." (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-1668_19m2.pdf)

Egbert v. Boule, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), decided June 8, 2022 by 6-3 (Justice Thomas writing), held that no Bivens damages remedy extends to Fourth Amendment excessive-force or First Amendment retaliation claims against federal agents. (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/596/21-147/)

Barnes v. Felix, 605 U.S. 73 (2025), decided May 15, 2025, in a unanimous opinion by Justice Kagan, rejected the Fifth Circuit's "moment-of-threat" rule, holding reasonableness must be judged on the "totality of the circumstances." (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/605/23-1239/)

Zorn v. Linton, No. 25-297 (March 23, 2026). In a 6-3 per curiam decision, the Court reversed the Second Circuit and granted immunity to Vermont Sergeant Jacob Zorn, holding the lower court failed to "identify a case where an officer taking similar actions in similar circumstances 'was held to have violated' the Constitution." (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/25-297)

Mullenix v. Luna, 577 U.S. 7 (2015) (officer who fatally shot a fleeing driver from an overpass granted immunity). (https://dlglearningcenter.com/scotus-officer-entitled-to-qualified-immunity-after-firing-shots-at-a-fleeing-vehicle/) Kisela v. Hughes, 584 U.S. 100 (2018) (officer who shot a woman holding a kitchen knife granted immunity). (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/17-467/)

The Second Circuit places the burden on the defendant, while the Fifth, Sixth, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, Eleventh, and D.C. Circuits place it on the plaintiff. (https://ackerman-ackerman.com/reflections-on-a-qualified-immunity-circuit-split/)

Colorado's SB 20-217 (signed June 19, 2020) created a state cause of action under Colo. Rev. Stat. § 13-21-131 and declared "[q]ualified immunity is not a defense to liability pursuant to this section." (https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb20-217) New Mexico's Civil Rights Act (HB 4, signed April 7, 2021) barred qualified immunity and caps liability at $2 million per claim. (https://innocenceproject.org/news/new-mexico-historic-legislation-to-end-qualified-immunity/)

The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act would amend § 1983 to bar the "not clearly established" defense. (https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/4991/text/is) The House passed H.R. 1280 by 220–212 on March 3, 2021. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualified_immunity)

William Baude, Is Qualified Immunity Unlawful?, 106 Calif. L. Rev. 45 (2018), found that of the roughly thirty qualified-immunity cases the Supreme Court decided between 1982 and 2018, it held officers violated clearly established law only twice. (https://www.cato.org/blog/supreme-court-wont-save-us-qualified-immunity) Joanna C. Schwartz, How Qualified Immunity Fails, 127 Yale L.J. 2 (2017), found only about 0.6% of cases were dismissed at the motion-to-dismiss stage on qualified-immunity grounds. (https://yalelawjournal.org/article/how-qualified-immunity-fails)

Justice Thomas wrote in his Baxter v. Bracey (2020) dissent "I continue to have strong doubts about our §1983 qualified immunity doctrine." (https://ij.org/press-release/supreme-court-refuses-to-hear-cases-challenging-qualified-immunity/) On June 15, 2020 the Court declined to hear eight qualified immunity cases. (https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/fair-courts-e-lert-us-supreme-court-declines-hear-qualified-immunity)
