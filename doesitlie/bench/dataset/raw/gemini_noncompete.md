State and Federal Standards for Enforcing Non-Compete Agreements: A Comprehensive Legal Analysis (2026)

Introduction: The Restrictive Covenant Landscape in the Modern Era

The legal architecture governing post-employment non-compete agreements has historically operated as a delicate balancing act within American jurisprudence. For decades, courts and legislatures have attempted to reconcile a corporate entity’s legitimate need to protect its intellectual property, trade secrets, and institutional goodwill with an individual employee’s fundamental right to workforce mobility and economic liberty. However, the period between 2023 and 2026 represents one of the most volatile and heavily litigated eras in the history of modern employment law. The landscape has fractured significantly, moving sharply away from a traditional, unified common law standard of reasonableness toward highly polarized, state-specific statutory frameworks.

1. The Federal Framework: The Rise, Litigation, and Collapse of the FTC Non-Compete Rule (2023–2026)

1.1 The Genesis and Scope of the 2024 FTC Rule

Under the direction of former Chair Lina Khan, the Federal Trade Commission sought to fundamentally alter the national discourse on restrictive covenants by promulgating the 2024 Non-Compete Clause Rule (https://www.icemiller.com/employment-independent-contractor-agreements/thought-leadership/federal-trade-commission-ends-appeals-shifts-focus-to-noncompete-enforcement). The rule was explicitly predicated on the economic theory that non-compete clauses keep wages artificially low, suppress new ideas, and rob the American economy of essential dynamism (https://www.icemiller.com/employment-independent-contractor-agreements/thought-leadership/federal-trade-commission-ends-appeals-shifts-focus-to-noncompete-enforcement). The sweeping regulation sought to ban nearly all post-employment non-compete agreements nationwide, preempting all conflicting state laws and invalidating millions of existing agreements by declaring them an "unfair method of competition" under Section 5 of the FTC Act (https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/rule-abandoned-crackdown-continues-the-federal-trade-commissions-new-non-compete-strategy/). Furthermore, the rule required employers to affirmatively rescind existing clauses and notify workers that their non-compete agreements were void, carving out only exceptionally narrow exceptions, such as for the bona fide sale of a business (https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/rule-abandoned-crackdown-continues-the-federal-trade-commissions-new-non-compete-strategy/).

1.2 Constitutional Pushback and Administrative Dissents

The promulgation of the rule was not unanimous within the FTC and immediately sparked fierce administrative and constitutional debate. Current Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson and Commissioner Melissa Holyoak dissented from the issuance of the rule, arguing that the FTC fundamentally lacked the statutory authority to issue substantive rules regarding unfair methods of competition (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/federal-trade-commission-files-accede-vacatur-non-compete-clause-rule). The dissenting commissioners articulated several core constitutional arguments against the agency's actions (https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2024-6-28-commissioner-holyoak-nc.pdf). The majority relied on Section 6(g) of the FTC Act as its source of authority, but the dissenters argued that Section 6(g) only authorizes procedural or internal operating rules, not substantive legal rules carrying the force of law (https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2024-6-28-commissioner-holyoak-nc.pdf). They also rejected the rule under the major questions doctrine, citing cases such as West Virginia v. EPA to point out that the Supreme Court remains highly skeptical of broad, unusual regulatory authority claimed through implicit delegations (https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2024-6-28-commissioner-holyoak-nc.pdf). Ultimately, the dissenters advocated for a return to the precedent established in the 1711 common law case Mitchel v. Reynolds, which highlighted the importance of evaluating non-competes on a case-by-case basis rather than issuing a sweeping ban (https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2024-6-28-commissioner-holyoak-nc.pdf).

1.3 Judicial Invalidation: The Ryan and Properties of the Villages Litigation

The defining death knell for the rule occurred in the United States District Court for the Northern District of Texas in the landmark litigation Ryan, LLC v. FTC (https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/rule-abandoned-crackdown-continues-the-federal-trade-commissions-new-non-compete-strategy/). In August 2024, Judge Ada E. Brown ruled that the FTC fundamentally lacked the statutory authority to promulgate the rule, deeming it arbitrary and capricious (https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/rule-abandoned-crackdown-continues-the-federal-trade-commissions-new-non-compete-strategy/). Consequently, the court issued a nationwide vacatur and injunction, halting the rule's implementation entirely (https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/rule-abandoned-crackdown-continues-the-federal-trade-commissions-new-non-compete-strategy/). Simultaneously, in Properties of the Villages v. FTC, a federal court in Florida issued a narrower, plaintiff-specific injunction against the rule, relying heavily on the major questions doctrine to find that the agency exceeded its authority (https://www.icemiller.com/employment-independent-contractor-agreements/thought-leadership/federal-trade-commission-ends-appeals-shifts-focus-to-noncompete-enforcement).

1.4 The 2025 FTC Pivot: Accession to Vacatur

On September 5, 2025, the FTC formally withdrew its notice of appeal in the Fifth Circuit (Ryan, LLC v. FTC, No. 24-10951) and the Eleventh Circuit (Properties of the Villages v. FTC, No. 24-13102), officially abandoning the challenge to the district court rulings (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/federal-trade-commission-files-accede-vacatur-non-compete-clause-rule). The decision to abandon the appeal and accede to the vacatur was finalized by a 3-1 vote of the Commission (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/12/2026-02866/revision-of-the-negative-option-rule-withdrawal-of-the-cars-rule-removal-of-the-non-compete-rule-to). In their accompanying public statements, Chairman Ferguson and Commissioner Holyoak emphasized that the FTC lacked authority for a sweeping rule and that the rule's illegality was "patently obvious" (https://www.kmm.com/ftc-abandons-federal-non-compete-ban/). Conversely, Commissioner Rebecca Kelly Slaughter was the sole dissenting vote, issuing a statement arguing that abandoning the appeals severely undermined essential worker protections (https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/ftc_abandons_appeals_decisions_striking_down_noncompete_rule_restrictive_covenants_remain_0925.html). The regulatory saga formally concluded when the FTC published a final rule in the Federal Register on February 12, 2026, officially removing the Non-Compete Rule from the Code of Federal Regulations, specifically 16 CFR part 910 (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/12/2026-02866/revision-of-the-negative-option-rule-withdrawal-of-the-cars-rule-removal-of-the-non-compete-rule-to).

1.5 Strategic Implications: A Return to Targeted Adjudication

On September 4, 2025, the day before withdrawing its appeals, the FTC filed a complaint against Gateway Pet Memorial Services, alleging that the company's widespread use of one-year non-competes in the pet cremation industry constituted an anticompetitive practice violating Section 5 of the FTC Act (https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/gateway-pet-memorial-services). Under the proposed consent order, finalized on November 25, 2025, Gateway was barred from enforcing non-competes against approximately 1,800 workers (https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/rule-abandoned-crackdown-continues-the-federal-trade-commissions-new-non-compete-strategy/). The order further forced the company to affirmatively notify employees that they were no longer bound by the agreements and imposed a strict 10-year compliance bar against entering into future non-competes with most employees (https://www.lawandtheworkplace.com/2025/09/ftc-signals-forthcoming-non-compete-enforcement-actions/).

2. California: The Absolute Prohibition Paradigm

2.1 The Controlling Statute: Business and Professions Code Section 16600

The bedrock of California's employment law is codified in California Business and Professions Code section 16600, which explicitly states that "every contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void" (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2023/12/california-strengthens-its-prohibitions-on-noncompetes-with-the-enactment-of-two-new-laws). The statute recognizes exceptionally few exceptions, applying strictly to the sale of the goodwill of a business (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code Section 16601), the dissolution of a partnership (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code Section 16602), or the dissolution of a limited liability company (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code Section 16602.5) (https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/california-extends-prohibition-on-noncompete-agreements.html).

2.2 The Leading Cases: Edwards and AMN Healthcare

The absolute nature of California's prohibition was decisively solidified by the California Supreme Court in the landmark case Edwards v. Arthur Andersen LLP, 44 Cal. 4th 937, decided on August 7, 2008 (https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2008/s147190/). The California Supreme Court categorically rejected the "narrow restraint" exception previously utilized by the Ninth Circuit, ruling that Section 16600 represents a strict per se standard of invalidity in the employment context, without any regard to the "reasonableness" of the restraint (https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2024/g061911.html). A decade later, the California Fourth District Court of Appeal drastically expanded this employee-friendly doctrine in AMN Healthcare, Inc. v. Aya Healthcare Servs., Inc., 28 Cal. App. 5th 923, decided in 2018 (https://www.fordharrison.com/non-compete-news-is-a-non-solicitation-of-employees-provision-enforceable-in-california). The court struck down the non-solicitation provision as void under Section 16600, holding that the clause impermissibly restrained the recruiters from engaging in their chosen profession of recruiting (https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/california-appellate-panel-affirms-injunction-blocking-use-of-employee-non-solicitation-provision-in-dispute-between-travel-nurse-providers.html). This decision, later reinforced by cases like Barker v. Insight Global, LLC, essentially invalidated employee non-solicitation provisions throughout the state (https://www.tradesecretsandemployeemobility.com/second-court-calls-into-question-viability-of-employee-non-solicitation-agreements).

2.3 The 2023–2024 Legislative Overhaul: SB 699 and AB 1076

Signed into law by Governor Gavin Newsom on September 1, 2023, and effective January 1, 2024, Senate Bill 699 added Section 16600.5 to the Business and Professions Code (https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB699/id/2839277). First, it explicitly extends the prohibition extraterritorially, specifying that any contract void under Section 16600 is unenforceable "regardless of where and when the contract was signed" (https://www.quinnemanuel.com/media/3b1nrqhz/client-alert_-new-california-non-compete-legislation.pdf). Legislative findings accompanying the bill forcefully declared that "California's public policy against restraint of trade law trumps other state laws... regardless of the persons' state of residence" (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2023/12/california-strengthens-its-prohibitions-on-noncompetes-with-the-enactment-of-two-new-laws). It creates a private right of action permitting employees, former employees, and prospective employees to sue employers for injunctive relief, actual damages, or both (https://www.quinnemanuel.com/media/3b1nrqhz/client-alert_-new-california-non-compete-legislation.pdf). Signed into law on October 13, 2023, and also effective January 1, 2024, Assembly Bill 1076 amended Section 16600 and added Section 16600.1 (https://www.californiaworkplacelawblog.com/2023/10/articles/california/new-california-law-makes-non-compete-agreements-unlawful-not-just-void/). Employers were legally mandated to send an individualized, written communication by February 14, 2024, to all current employees and former employees who were employed after January 1, 2022, explicitly notifying them that the non-compete provisions in their contracts were void (https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/california-extends-prohibition-on-noncompete-agreements.html). The notice had to be delivered to the last known address and the email address of the employee (https://legiscan.com/CA/text/AB1076/id/2833265).

3. New York: The Common Law Reasonableness Standard and Legislative Gridlock

3.2 The Leading Cases: Reed, Roberts and BDO Seidman

The modern framework for assessing non-competes in New York was firmly established in Reed, Roberts Assoc. v. Strauman, 40 N.Y.2d 303, decided by the New York Court of Appeals in 1976 (https://www.ottingerlaw.com/blog/non-compete/new-york-non-compete-core-concepts-the-reed-roberts-decision/). In this case, Reed Roberts Associates provided advice to employers regarding unemployment laws and had over 6,000 customers (https://www.ottingerlaw.com/blog/non-compete/new-york-non-compete-core-concepts-the-reed-roberts-decision/). Reed, Roberts established the definitive four-prong "Reasonableness Framework" (https://www.nycourts.gov/LegacyPDFS/courts/comdiv/PDFs/7th%20JD%20DOIs/Nalge%20v.Warren%20DEC.pdf). This standard was further refined in the 1999 landmark decision BDO Seidman v. Hirshberg, 93 N.Y.2d 382 (https://law.justia.com/cases/new-york/court-of-appeals/1999/93-n-y-2d-382-0.html). Crucially, BDO Seidman firmly entrenched the precedent for "blue-penciling" or partial enforcement in New York (https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/employment-2025/usa-new-york/trends-and-developments). Rather than voiding the entire agreement as the lower appellate division had done, the court severed the invalid portions, enforcing an 18-month restriction solely to protect the specific client relationships the accountant had acquired and developed through his employment with BDO Seidman (https://law.justia.com/cases/new-york/court-of-appeals/1999/93-n-y-2d-382-0.html).

3.3 The 2023–2026 Legislative Gridlock

On June 20, 2023, the New York State Legislature passed Senate Bill S3100, a sweeping legislative overhaul intended to mimic California's absolute prohibition (https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/blogs/2026/April/New-York-Senate-Re-Introduces-Bill-Prohibit-Certain-Non-Compete-Agreements). Following intense lobbying from Wall Street and the financial sector, Governor Kathy Hochul vetoed the bill on December 22, 2023 (https://www.goldbergsegalla.com/news-and-knowledge/knowledge/what-ny-state-employers-need-to-know-about-2024-employment-law/). In a targeted response to the governor's veto, Senator Sean Ryan introduced a revised bill, S4641, on February 10, 2025 (https://www.skadden.com/-/media/files/publications/2025/08/new_york_restrictive_covenant_update.pdf?rev=66b05f87ab3b40598f05af9d7fd5c085). This compromised legislation applied only prospectively and specifically exempted "highly compensated individuals" whose average annualized rate of cash compensation during the past three years was $500,000 or more (https://www.skadden.com/-/media/files/publications/2025/08/new_york_restrictive_covenant_update.pdf?rev=66b05f87ab3b40598f05af9d7fd5c085). Despite passing the Senate on June 9, 2025, S4641 died in the Assembly Labor Committee before the legislative session ended (https://www.skadden.com/-/media/files/publications/2025/08/new_york_restrictive_covenant_update.pdf?rev=66b05f87ab3b40598f05af9d7fd5c085). The legislation was resurrected on April 6, 2026, when Senator Michael Gianaris re-introduced the substantively identical text as Senate Bill S9759 (https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/blogs/2026/April/New-York-Senate-Re-Introduces-Bill-Prohibit-Certain-Non-Compete-Agreements).

4. Texas: The Ancillary Requirement and Healthcare Industry Reforms

4.1 The Controlling Statute: Business and Commerce Code Section 15.50(a)

The enforceability of general non-compete agreements in Texas is governed exclusively by the Texas Free Enterprise and Antitrust Act of 1983, specifically codified at Texas Business & Commerce Code Section 15.50(a) (https://www.employmentlawspotlight.com/blogs/new-decade-same-rules-noncompetes-in-texas-10-years-after-marsh-usa-inc-v-cook/). First, the covenant not to compete must be "ancillary to or part of an otherwise enforceable agreement at the time the agreement is made" (https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2011/2001724.html).

4.2 The Leading Cases: Alex Sheshunoff and Marsh USA

Historically, under the 1994 case Light v. Centel Cellular Co. of Tex., 883 S.W. 2d 642, the Texas Supreme Court utilized a highly restrictive standard (https://commons.stmarytx.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2665&context=thestmaryslawjournal). However, in the seminal 2006 case Alex Sheshunoff Management Services, L.P. v. Johnson, 209 S.W.3d 644, the Texas Supreme Court substantially relaxed this rigid interpretation (https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2011/2001724.html). This was further expanded in 2009 by Mann Frankfort Stein & Lipp Advisors, Inc. v. Fielding, 289 S.W.3d 844, which allowed for implied promises of confidential information to satisfy the statute (https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/2:2014cv00265/1185969/32/). The nature of valid consideration was further revolutionized in 2011 by Marsh USA Inc. v. Cook, 354 S.W.3d 764 (https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/thirteenth-court-of-appeals/2016/13-14-00437-cv.html). The Texas Supreme Court ruled for the first time that the award of stock options to key employees serves as proper and sufficient consideration to support a non-compete (https://www.gdhm.com/news-post/texas-supreme-court-gives-employers-greater-independence-to-enter-into-non-competes/). Crucially, however, despite the liberalization in Marsh, subsequent Texas jurisprudence firmly maintains that a pure financial payment—such as a cash bonus, a salary raise, or a severance payout—is not sufficient consideration to support a restrictive covenant in Texas (https://www.employmentlawspotlight.com/blogs/new-decade-same-rules-noncompetes-in-texas-10-years-after-marsh-usa-inc-v-cook/).

4.3 The 2025 Healthcare Reforms: Senate Bill 1318

On June 20, 2025, Governor Greg Abbott signed Senate Bill 1318 into law, imposing sweeping, hardline restrictions on healthcare covenants effective September 1, 2025 (https://www.fordharrison.com/changes-to-texas-noncompete-rules-for-physicians-and-certain-other-healthcare-providers-effective-september-1-2025). SB 1318 amends the existing physician regulations under Section 15.50(b) and adds a completely new Section 15.501, establishing extreme structural limits on enforcement (https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB01318F.htm). The restricted area cannot exceed a five-mile radius from the specific location where the practitioner primarily practiced prior to the termination of their contract (https://www.boyarmiller.com/five-key-changes-to-physician-non-compete-agreements-in-texas/). Replaces the previous "reasonable price" arbitration standard with a strict statutory cap: the buyout amount cannot be greater than the practitioner's total annual salary and wages at the time of termination (https://www.boyarmiller.com/five-key-changes-to-physician-non-compete-agreements-in-texas/). Under Section 15.50(d), a covenant is entirely void and unenforceable if the physician is involuntarily discharged without "good cause" (https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/GetStatute.aspx?Code=BC&Value=15.50).

5. Comparative Cross-Jurisdictional Analysis

The collapse of the Federal Trade Commission's national rulemaking experiment in early 2026 created a massive regulatory vacuum, forcing the structural evolution of non-compete enforcement permanently back into the hands of state legislatures and local judiciaries (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/12/2026-02866/revision-of-the-negative-option-rule-withdrawal-of-the-cars-rule-removal-of-the-non-compete-rule-to). California's aggressive codification of extraterritorial application under SB 699 creates profound operational risks (https://www.quinnemanuel.com/media/3b1nrqhz/client-alert_-new-california-non-compete-legislation.pdf). 2025's SB 1318 implemented rigid caps (1-year duration, 5-mile radius) and salary-based buyout limits for healthcare providers (https://www.tradesecretsandemployeemobility.com/texas-amends-restrictive-covenant-laws-for-healthcare-providers).
