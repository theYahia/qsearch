# The Fair Use Doctrine in U.S. Copyright Law (Claude DR)

Section 107 lists six favored example purposes in its preamble (criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, research) and four factors: purpose/character, nature of the work, amount/substantiality used, and market effect. (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)

Transformative use originated in Judge Pierre Leval's 1990 Harvard Law Review article and was adopted by the Supreme Court in Campbell (1994). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toward_a_Fair_Use_Standard)

Google v. Oracle (2021) found fair use for copying ~11,500 lines of Java API declaring code (0.4% of the program), assuming copyrightability without deciding it. (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/18-956_d18f.pdf)

Warhol v. Goldsmith (2023, 7–2) held the first factor disfavored the Warhol Foundation because its commercial licensing of "Orange Prince" shared substantially the same purpose as Goldsmith's photograph. (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2023/05/andy-warhol-foundation-for-the-visual-arts-inc-v-goldsmith)

Fair use is codified at Section 107 of the Copyright Act of 1976 (Pub. L. 94-553, enacted Oct. 19, 1976). (https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USCODE-2010-title17/USCODE-2010-title17-chap1-sec107) The statute provides that "the fair use of a copyrighted work … for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship, or research, is not an infringement of copyright." (https://www.bitlaw.com/source/17usc/107.html)

The six illustrative favored purposes are criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. (https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/)

Congress intended Section 107 to "restate the present judicial doctrine of fair use, not to change, narrow, or enlarge it in any way," describing fair use as "an equitable rule of reason." (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)

The Supreme Court in Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises (1985) called the fourth factor "undoubtedly the single most important element of fair use." (https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Harper_%26_Row_v._Nation_Enterprises/Opinion_of_the_Court)

Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), decided March 7, 1994, a unanimous decision by Justice Souter, held that a commercial parody can be fair use. (https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/510/569/) The Court asked "whether and to what extent the new work is 'transformative'" — whether it "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-1292.ZO.html) The Court stated "the more transformative the new work, the less will be the significance of other factors, like commercialism." (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-1292.ZO.html)

The transformative-use concept derived from Judge Leval's 1990 article "Toward a Fair Use Standard," 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1105. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toward_a_Fair_Use_Standard)

Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. 1 (2021), decided April 5, 2021, in a 6–2 opinion by Justice Breyer, held that Google's copying of approximately 11,500 lines of declaring code was fair use as a matter of law. (https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/18-956_d18f.pdf) The 11,500 lines were just 0.4% of the entire 2.86-million-line Java SE program. (https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10597)

Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), decided May 18, 2023, in a 7–2 opinion by Justice Sotomayor, held that the first fair use factor did not favor the Warhol Foundation's 2016 commercial licensing of "Orange Prince" to Condé Nast. (https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2023/5/supreme-court-decides-andy-warhol-foundation-for-the-visual-arts-inc-v-goldsmith) The Court clarified that Campbell "cannot be read to mean that the first factor weighs in favor of any use that adds some new expression, meaning or message." (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2023/05/andy-warhol-foundation-for-the-visual-arts-inc-v-goldsmith)

Professor Barton Beebe's empirical study found that of the core opinions in which factor one favored fair use, 92.2% ultimately found fair use, while of those in which factor one disfavored fair use, 96.6% found no fair use. (https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Liu_20190203.pdf)

The U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 3 (May 9, 2025), concluded AI training is not categorically fair use. (https://copyrightalliance.org/copyright-offices-ai-report-takeaways/) The report warned that "making commercial use of vast troves of copyrighted works to produce expressive content that competes with them in existing markets … goes beyond established fair use boundaries." (https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/us-copyright-office-releases-third-report-on-ai-and-copyright-addressing-training-ai-models-with-copyrighted-materials)

Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (D. Del., Feb. 11, 2025). Judge Stephanos Bibas granted partial summary judgment for Thomson Reuters, holding Ross's copying of Westlaw headnotes to train its AI legal-research tool was not fair use. (https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/thomson-reuters-enter-centre-gmbh-et-al-v-ross-intelligence-inc.html) The court found Ross copied at least 2,243 headnotes. (https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/court-ai-fair-use-thomson-reuters-enterprise-gmbh-ross-intelligence/)

Bartz v. Anthropic PBC (N.D. Cal., June 23, 2025). Judge William Alsup held Anthropic's use of books to train its Claude LLMs was "exceedingly transformative" and fair use, but downloading pirated books was not. (https://www.wiggin.com/publication/bartz-v-anthropic-first-court-decision-on-fair-use-defense-in-llm-training/) Anthropic agreed to pay a minimum of $1.5 billion into a settlement fund. (https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/09/anthropics-landmark-copyright-settlement-implications-for-ai-developers-and-enterprise-users) Judge Alsup granted preliminary approval on September 25, 2025. (https://authorsguild.org/advocacy/artificial-intelligence/what-authors-need-to-know-about-the-anthropic-settlement/)

Kadrey v. Meta Platforms, Inc. (N.D. Cal., June 25, 2025). Judge Vince Chhabria granted summary judgment to Meta on fair use. (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/kadrey-v-meta-platforms-inc) He identified a "potentially winning" theory: market dilution. (https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-n-d-cal/117422847.html)

New York Times Co. v. Microsoft & OpenAI (S.D.N.Y.). On April 4, 2025, Judge Sidney Stein denied most of OpenAI's and Microsoft's motions to dismiss. (https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2025-04/yf%2023cv11195%20OpenAI%20MTD%20opinion%20april%204%202025.pdf)

Getty Images v. Stability AI (UK High Court, Nov. 4, 2025). Justice Joanna Smith largely rejected Getty's claims, dismissing the secondary copyright infringement claim. (https://www.lw.com/en/insights/getty-images-v-stability-ai-english-high-court-rejects-secondary-copyright-claim)
